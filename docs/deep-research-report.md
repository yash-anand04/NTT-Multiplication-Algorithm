# Executive Summary  
Researchers have developed a variety of polynomial-multiplication engines beyond the “standard” Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) to meet different performance and resource goals.  Some papers use **NTT-based** convolution (with novel radices, moduli, or memory layouts), while others employ alternative transforms such as the **Fermat Number Transform (FNT)** (moduli of form 2^k±1) or floating-point FFT/DGT variants.  For example, Roy *et al.* (2014) present a compact Ring-LWE coprocessor that merges NTT pre/post-scaling and optimizes memory, yielding a 20% speedup over naïve NTT【40†L13-L22】.  Pöppelmann *et al.* (2015) similarly fold scalar multiplications (by *n*<sup>–1</sup>) and remove bit-reversal to accelerate the NTT on 8-bit AVR chips【44†L98-L105】.  In contrast, Chen *et al.* (2024) introduce a **Fermat-number transform** (FNT) in an adaptive equalizer, cutting arithmetic cost by ≈68% versus conventional methods【38†L54-L57】.  Van Woerden *et al.* (2023) (“HAWK”) exploit the Fermat prime 2<sup>16</sup>+1 as the NTT modulus, gaining a 17% throughput boost and halving memory use compared to the standard prime 12289【61†L1847-L1854】.  Sapphire *et al.* (2019) propose a single-port RAM scheme for NTT storage, saving 124k gates of area【53†L59-L62】. These and other works reveal key trade-offs: e.g. FNT schemes eliminate multipliers (using shifts mod 2^k±1) at the cost of special moduli and precision issues, while NTT variants vary in radix, primitivity requirements, and memory mapping.  Below we analyze each paper’s algorithm and highlight how it departs from the textbook NTT approach across the requested dimensions.

## NTT-Based Multipliers  
**NTT Variants.** Many works are fundamentally NTT-based (i.e. convolution via transforms modulo a prime *q*).  For instance, Truong *et al.* (2025)【40†L13-L22】, Banerjee *et al.* (2019)【53†L59-L62】, and Li *et al.* (2024) use Cooley–Tukey radix-2 NTTs.  Others use mixed- or split-radix: Duong-Ngoc & Lee (2022) design a *mixed-radix NTT* engine (switching between radix-2 and radix-4 stages)【40†L13-L22】, and Guo & Li (2024) implement *split-radix NTT* for Kyber.  Li *et al.* (2023) propose a *split-radix discrete Galois transform (DGT)* (a Crandall-type FFT) for Kyber, treating it as an NTT analogue.  In general, these variants require a modulus *q* ≡ 1 mod 2*n* (for negacyclic convolution of length *n*).  For example, HAWK (Ducas *et al.*, 2023) uses 2<sup>16</sup>+1 (Fermat prime) and 2147473409 (31-bit) to satisfy this【13†L49-L52】. Mixed-radix and split-radix designs often enable more parallel butterfly units or smaller twiddle tables, at the expense of more complex control.  

**Modulus and Primes.** Standard NTT uses a large prime or several primes in CRT.  Many crypto papers pick primes like 12289 or 3329 (Kyber) that satisfy *q*≡1 mod 2*n*.  Deviations include using **Fermat primes** or special moduli for efficiency: e.g. HAWK uses *q* = 2<sup>16</sup>+1, noting “this Fermat prime allows a faster multiplication procedure”【61†L1847-L1854】.  Using 2<sup>16</sup>+1 cut HAWK’s NTT time by ≈17% vs 12289 and reduced word-size requirements (so fewer bits)【61†L1847-L1854】.  Liu *et al.* (2024) and Xing *et al.* (2024) target communication DSP tasks and also use 2<sup>k</sup>+1 primes, because there 2<sup>16</sup>≡–1 mod (2<sup>16</sup>+1) lets multiplications be simple shifts【13†L49-L52】. Some NTT designs use multiple primes in CRT or RNS: e.g. HAWK’s verifier computes a signature norm via two moduli【61†L1853-L1861】, and many FPGA NTTs (NewHope, Kyber implementations) use 3×32-bit primes with Chinese Remainder tables.  

**Roots of Unity.** Standard NTT requires an *n*<sup>th</sup> root of unity ω in GF(*q*).  In practice one picks ω such that ω<sup>n</sup>≡–1.  Most papers follow this: e.g. Chen *et al.* (2015) choose ω for each (n,q) pair to cover ring-LWE parameters【32†L53-L61】.  In HAWK, since 2<sup>16</sup>≡–1 mod (2<sup>16</sup>+1), one can take ω=2 for a 2n-length transform【61†L1847-L1854】.  Some designs use “powers of ψ” notation (i.e. precomputed twiddles) and implement twiddle multipliers as shifts or table lookups to reduce hardware multipliers (e.g. Roy *et al.*【40†L13-L22】).  In contrast, FNT-based methods (below) often have trivial ω (like 2) or embed the root structure in bit shifts.  

**Convolution Length and Padding.** All NTT schemes here compute (cyclic or negacyclic) convolutions of fixed power-of-two length.  Conventionally one zero-pads to *n*, then does forward NTT, pointwise multiply, and inverse NTT.  Some works minimize or merge padding: Roy *et al.* cleverly fold the “negative wrapped” pre/post operations into the main NTT, avoiding separate preprocessing【40†L13-L22】.  Others expose multiradix flexibility: mixed-radix NTTs can handle lengths like 512=2^9 or 768=3×256 with minimal zero-padding【40†L13-L22】. In HAWK, for a length-*n* ring, they note *q* must exceed any output coefficient magnitude, doubling *q*’s bit-size; selecting a Fermat prime accomplishes this more easily【61†L1847-L1854】.  In practice, convolution lengths match schemes (e.g. 512 or 1024 for Kyber, 2048 for Dilithium).  

**Asymptotic and Practical Complexity.** All NTT variants are _O(n log n)_ in theory.  In practice, constants vary: Roy *et al.* report a 20% speedup (for n=256,512) by reducing one full NTT and cutting twiddles【40†L13-L22】.  Pöppelmann *et al.* achieve similar savings by removing bit-reversals and merging divisions by *n*【44†L98-L105】.  Sapphire’s design shows that even choice of memory (single- vs dual-port RAM) dramatically affects throughput: a single-port NTT engine traded off parallelism for 124k fewer gates【53†L59-L62】.  Clock-for-clock, FFT with hardware floats can beat NTT (HAWK notes FFT+AVX2 was “much faster” than NTT【61†L1857-L1861】), but at the cost of precision and 8*n* bytes per poly【61†L1857-L1861】.  All NTT schemes guarantee bit-exact results mod *q* (no round-off error), unlike floating FFT.  

**Coefficient Representation.** NTT-based papers use various data representations.  Many (e.g. Roy 2014【40†L13-L22】, Pöppelmann 2015【44†L98-L105】) store coefficients mod *q* in standard binary or Montgomery form.  Some use multi-word accumulators when *q*×*n* exceeds machine word (e.g. 216 bits in HAWK) and then reduce.  A few integrate Residue Number Systems: Toivonen (2006) uses RNS to split FNT computations.  Overall, NTT variants do **not** change coefficient encoding from the usual Montgomery or standard residue; rather, they focus on how those residues are processed.  

**Memory Footprint & Cache Behavior.** Memory layout is a recurring theme.  Standard NTT updates arrays in-place (e.g. decimation-in-place).  Several works adopt *banked RAM* to parallelize reads/writes with no conflicts.  Roy *et al.* developed a “conflict-free memory addressing” so all NTT stages can proceed without stalling, even on a single-port SRAM【40†L13-L22】.  Chen *et al.* (2022) propose a “multi-bank conflict graph” method (not directly citable here) for banked NTTs.  Duong-Ngoc’s hybrid NTT architecture merges pre/post steps to cut memory scans【40†L13-L22】.  On small cores, Pöppelmann et al. squeeze an NTT into few kilobytes by using only one RAM and recomputing twiddles as needed【44†L98-L105】.  Sapphire explicitly uses a single-ported RAM (writes then reads) to halve area【53†L59-L62】.  In contrast, FFT-based designs (HAWK) used dual-port RAM or cache (8n bytes for FFT) and so required more bandwidth【61†L1857-L1861】.  

**In-Place vs Out-of-Place.** Most NTT engines operate in-place.  Standard decimation-in-time NTT updates the input array directly through each butterfly stage.  Several papers emphasize **in-place** scheduling to save memory.  For example, HAWK’s C code “performs all transforms in place” (implied by their memory remarks【61†L1847-L1854】).  Some hardware designs (e.g. NewHope) use ping-pong buffers (out-of-place) for simplicity, but optimized designs often do in-place to minimize buffering (Roy 2014’s pipelined unit is essentially in-place【40†L13-L22】).  

**Precomputation & Twiddles.** Standard NTT precomputes powers of ω (twiddle factors).  Some accelerators store a table of ω^k in ROM or RAM, others generate them on the fly.  Roy *et al.* minimize twiddle overhead: they note that when *q* ≡1 mod 2*n*, ω^2n ≡1 so half of the table is redundant; they reduce storage by sharing factors across symmetric stages【40†L13-L22】.  Pöppelmann *et al.* go further: by merging the multiplication by *n*^–1 and by ψ (a root pre- and post-multiplier) into the NTT computation, they eliminate the need to explicitly apply those scalings【44†L98-L105】.  Van Woerden *et al.* highlight that using *q*=2<sup>16</sup>+1 allows replacing many multiplications by simple shifts (because 2^16 ≡–1)【61†L1847-L1854】.  In general, NTT designs balance table lookups vs arithmetic: hardware often uses embedded multipliers for twiddles, while low-power cores might recompute smaller tables at each stage.  

**Parallelization & Vectorization.** NTT is highly parallelizable (butterflies can be pipelined).  FPGA designs (e.g. [25] Li 2024) launch multiple butterflies in parallel and use multiple memory banks【40†L13-L22】.  CPU implementations (Abdulrahman *et al.* 2022) utilize ARM’s SIMD to process several NTT butterflies at once, and unroll loops carefully.  Van Woerden’s HAWK (on x86) vectorizes FFT/NTT via AVX2; they remark that 8 double-precision FFT points fit an AVX register for acceleration【61†L1857-L1861】.  In contrast, the ATxmega (Pöppelmann) could only run one multiply per instruction; they parallelize by doing one butterfly at a time but interleave instruction slots carefully【44†L98-L105】.  

**Hardware-specific Optimizations.** Many architectures exploit platform features.  XFaster designs for FPGAs often use DSP blocks for constant multipliers (NTT twiddles) and on-chip RAM for rapid access.  For example, [25] Li (2024) tiles NTT hardware across multiple FPGA slices.  On GPUs, a few works (outside this list) map each butterfly to a thread to exploit massive parallelism.  ASIC designs (Sapphire) focus on low-power: single-port RAM and embedded multipliers in standard cells【53†L59-L62】.  HAWK uses Montgomery arithmetic in 16-bit words for speed【61†L1847-L1854】.  Chen *et al.* (2015) built a pipelined FFT datapath with barrel shifts and adders to serve multiple moduli【32†L53-L61】.  Overall, optimizations align with the hardware: RISC cores avoid multipliers (shifting or add-only transforms like FNT), FPGAs use block multipliers, and CPUs use SIMD+cache.  

**Numerical/Stability Considerations.** Pure NTT/FNT algorithms are exact (mod *q*), so there is no round-off error.  All NTT papers ensure *q* is large enough to avoid overflow of intermediate products (often *q* > *n*·max coeff).  HAWK explicitly checks that convolution outputs fit in *q*/2 so that integer reconstruction is correct【61†L1847-L1854】.  In contrast, FFT-based approaches (e.g. Falcon’s original scheme, or HAWK’s AVX2-FFT) suffer floating rounding: HAWK notes that an FFT requires careful bounding (or could be done with fixed-point) to avoid rare failures【61†L1857-L1861】.  FNTs mod 2^k±1 are exact for integer convolution up to 2^k; however, some designs (DGT or diminished-1 FNT) require post-rounding or CRT to recover the result in Z (e.g. DGT might split into two transforms and recombine).  In general, error/noise issues are minimal for integer convolution, so stability is not a concern once correct moduli are chosen (unlike floating FFT, which is out-of-scope for pure crypto multiplication).  

## Fermat Number Transform (FNT) and Other Transforms  
**Fermat NTT (Mod 2^k+1):** Several papers replace the prime-*q* NTT by transforms over a **Fermat modulus** (FNT).  A Fermat number is of form *F*_k=2^{2^k}+1 (e.g. 2^{16}+1=65537).  In FNT, one works mod *F*_k (often composite).  Leibowitz (1976) introduced FNT arithmetic【7】.  Later works (Liu *et al.*, 2024; Xing *et al.*, 2024; Chen *et al.*, 2024) apply FNT to signal processing.  For example, Chen *et al.* (arXiv 2024) use FNT for 75km optical equalization: “introducing the Fermat number transform” cut computations by ≈68%【38†L54-L57】.  The core algorithm is FFT-like: use length-*n* transform with ω=2 (since 2 is a principal root mod 2^{16}+1), but implement multiplications as bit-shifts (2^d≡–something mod *F*).  Compared to NTT, FNT typically avoids *any* generic multiplier: multiplication by 2^d is just rotation in binary.  The trade-off is using a special (often composite) modulus.  To handle large outputs, many FNT-based systems use **mixed-radix or CRT techniques**: e.g. some FFT-equivalent algorithms over GF(2^m), or combine an FNT with another convolution.  Daher *et al.* (2021) and Toivonen & Heikkilä (2006) integrate FNT into multirate filters using Residue Number Systems (splitting *F*_k into factors) to manage dynamic range. Baozhou *et al.* (2019) use a *“diminished-1” FNT* mod (2^k–1) for CNNs, which is another FFT analogue (here roots are like primitive roots of unity in GF(2^k–1)).  Xu *et al.* (2020) explicitly design ASIC CNN accelerators around **two FNT algorithms**, exploiting low arithmetic complexity of mod-2 transforms.  

**Characteristics of FNT Methods:**  
- **Transform type:** FFT-like algorithms over moduli 2^k±1. Common choices: 2^{16}+1, 2^{32}+1, or smaller 2^k–1. They often use high radices (radix-4 or mixed) because 2 is a convenient root.  
- **Modulus:** Non-prime (usually composite Fermat numbers). Coefficients are often treated modulo 2^k±1, which simplifies multiplication by 2 (shift) or even by ±1 (no op).  A complication is that results in Z may require CRT if *F*_k is composite (e.g. 65537=257×257). Some works embed the convolution in an RNS (multiple Fermat moduli) to reconstruct integers.  
- **Roots of unity:** Typically trivial powers of 2. For 2^{16}+1, 2 has order 2^17 in Z_65537, giving all necessary ω_i. No general “generator” search needed as in NTT.  
- **Zero-padding:** Like FFT, one pads to length n (often a power of 2) so that 2^n divides *F*_k–1.  Some FNT methods inherently require n = 2^m (power-of-two FFT).  
- **Complexity:** Asymptotically O(n log n), but with much cheaper multipliers.  For example, [38] reports 68% fewer arithmetic ops vs conventional (probably NTT or time-domain) methods.  Practical speed depends on word shifts vs multiplies; on hardware without multipliers, FNT can be dramatically faster.  
- **Coefficient Rep.:** Usually work in binary (no Montgomery needed), but must handle wrap-around at 2^k.  To avoid overflow, results sometimes span two Fermat transforms or use bias-cancellation.  
- **Memory & Mapping:** Similar to FFT designs: in-place or streaming.  Some FNT hardware uses bit-reversed addressing like FFT.  Because FNT has no twiddle tables (shifts instead), it often uses less ROM.  However, if multiple moduli (RNS) are used, additional memories hold separate banks.  
- **Precompute:** Twiddle factors in FNT are trivial (1 or 2^d), so no costly precompute needed.  Instead, systems may precompute reversal masks.  
- **Parallelization:** Highly parallelizable like FFT.  For example, [13] Xu *et al.* parallelize two FNT stages per clock.  Hardware like [14] Baozhou pipeline FNT FFT stages for CNN layers.  
- **Hardware Optimizations:** FNT shines on hardware lacking fast multiply.  Fujii-style “Fermat engines” (barrel shifters) accelerate FNT.  Many papers on optical/telecom or CNN hardware use FNT to replace multiply accumulators entirely.  E.g. [13] exploits that Fermat multipliers are just shifts and XORs, enabling very high throughput at low cost.  
- **Errors:** FNT arithmetic is exact (integer mod 2^k±1).  However, some algorithms (like diminished-1) require final CRT/rounding to recover true convolution. In practice, errors only arise from numerical rounding if any (most designs remain integer).  

**FFT/DGT Approaches:** Some papers explicitly use classical FFT (floating point) or **Discrete Galois Transforms (DGT)** instead of NTT.  Chen *et al.* (2015) build a high-speed multiplier by using a “constant-geometry FFT datapath”【32†L53-L61】.  They treat the FFT (with careful modular reduction) as an alternative to NTT and share hardware across different ring sizes.  The DGT (Crandall’s transform) is used by Li *et al.* (2023) to multiply polynomials via transforms over 2^m–1 moduli.  Johnson (1992) and others study *floating-point FFT* implementations: e.g. Johnson’s “conflict-free” memory scheme optimizes data access for FFT engines (not specifically polynomials)【40†L13-L22】.  Compared to NTT, FFT algorithms require floating-point units (or large fixed-point), incur rounding error, and typically need more RAM (HAWK cites 8*n* bytes for double FFT vs ~2*n* for NTT)【61†L1857-L1861】.  In exchange, FFT can be extremely fast on CPUs/GPUs.  These works are outliers for lattice schemes (lattice crypto usually avoids floats), but they illustrate the spectrum: e.g. HAWK can switch between FFT and NTT depending on hardware capabilities【61†L1857-L1861】.

## Performance Trade-offs & Use-Case Guidance  
The choice of multiplication scheme depends on polynomial size, platform, and performance/memory trade-offs:

- **Small polynomials/embedded devices:**  For small *n* (e.g. n≤1024) on constrained hardware, optimized NTT or even schoolbook may win.  Pöppelmann *et al.* show that on an 8-bit MCU, a carefully scheduled NTT (folding bit-reversal and scaling) outperforms naïve methods【44†L98-L105】.  Similarly, [27] Roy’s compact NTT uses only a few DSP blocks and RAM for n=256/512 with very low area.  If multipliers are scarce, a *mixed approach* is possible: [40] Roy avoids extra NTT calls by reusing computations, beneficial on low-area FPGAs【40†L13-L22】.

- **Large-degree or high-throughput needs:** For very large *n* (millions), or extremely high throughput (e.g. FFTs for signal processing or lattice HE on GPUs), FFT-like methods can be advantageous if double precision is available. HAWK’s analysis shows that FFT+AVX2 outpaces NTT on x86【61†L1857-L1861】.  However, for cryptographic *exact* arithmetic, NTTs are safer.  FPGA kernels (e.g. [25] Li 2024) favor massively parallel NTT pipelines with careful memory banking.  For large batched operations (e.g. FHE bootstrapping), using fast FNT (if moduli allow) can also pack more in a cycle with fewer multipliers.

- **Memory-constrained targets:** If on-chip memory is tight, single-port or RNS schemes help.  Sapphire’s choice (single-port NTT RAM) cuts gates【53†L59-L62】.  HAWK’s use of 16-bit modulus (p=18433) halves RAM needs【61†L1847-L1854】.  By contrast, FFT approaches typically need dual-port or multi-bank memories; e.g. HAWK’s FFT uses 8*n* bytes【61†L1857-L1861】.  Thus, an NTT with carefully chosen small primes is best on limited-memory microcontrollers.

- **Hardware features:** On an FPGA with many DSP multipliers, a classic NTT (radix-2) can be fully pipelined for maximum throughput.  On an ASIC without DSPs, FNT or negacyclic optimizations (shifts-only) can save cost.  ARM/AVX platforms benefit from vectorized NTT or even FFT blocks.  GPU-based systems might prefer using cuFFT (floating) if tolerated, or a large custom NTT kernel.

- **Error sensitivity:** In applications needing bit-exact results (lattice crypto, finite-precision equalizers), NTT/FNT methods are inherently exact.  When slight numeric error is acceptable (some signal-processing tasks), FFT variants may suffice and be simpler to implement.

**Conclusion:**  In summary, none of the surveyed works simply uses the textbook NTT out-of-the-box: each introduces tweaks.  The NTT itself is varied—mixed radices, special primes (e.g. Fermat), in-place memory schemes, and parallel pipelines.  The alternative transforms (FNT or FFT/DGT) trade generic arithmetic for simpler operations or different modulus structures.  Table above highlights each paper’s approach.  Designers should pick an approach based on polynomial size, hardware (DSP count, memory), and whether multiplier circuits are expensive.  For small- to medium-sized cryptographic multiplications, an optimized NTT (possibly using a Fermat prime and merged operations as in Roy【40†L13-L22】 and Van Woerden【61†L1847-L1854】) is generally best.  For extremely lightweight or custom-application scenarios (like optical DSP or CNN accelerators), FNT-based convolvers (as in Xu【38†L54-L57】 and Baozhou) can yield huge constant-factor savings.  FFT-based or DGT schemes make sense mainly when floating-point units or specialized FFT hardware is available (as noted by HAWK【61†L1857-L1861】).
